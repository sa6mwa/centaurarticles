# The Coordination Trap

*AI is changing what people can accomplish. Companies are still paying for the organization that gets in their way.*

When I read [Computer Sweden’s report that AI was producing no clear gains for most companies](https://computersweden.se/article/4240298/tung-ekonom-ai-ger-inga-tydliga-vinster-for-de-flesta-foretag.html), my reaction was frustration rather than surprise. Of course the returns are disappointing when companies keep paying for the coordination that prevents the capability from becoming useful. Over the past year, I have watched the same pattern repeat: work becomes technically possible, the people capable of doing it are available, and progress disappears into decisions, permissions and negotiations over responsibility. The company pays for the people, the technology and the waiting, then questions the value of the technology.

I developed [*The Coordination Shift*](https://c89.systems/10x.pdf) in September 2025 because the economics of the work I was doing had changed. A capable engineer working with AI could carry a substantially larger and more coherent body of work than the organizational models around us assumed. I expected those models to take time to adapt. What has become difficult to tolerate is the willingness to treat the resulting obstruction as normal, even while demanding more productivity from everyone caught inside it.

Two recent articles bring that frustration into focus. Torsten Slok describes the missing financial payoff. Justine Hastings, Chad Syverson and Seth Zimmerman examine the coordination burden inside companies already using AI. Read together, they suggest a more consequential explanation than insufficient adoption: enterprises are buying new productive capabilities while preserving the arrangements that prevent them from using those capabilities fully. The solution requires changing those arrangements, including who owns decisions, how information moves and what management is rewarded for maintaining.

## The missing return has an organizational address

The financial observation behind the Computer Sweden story is straightforward. In his October assessment, [Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok, as reported by Barron’s](https://www.barrons.com/livecoverage/sunday1025/card/apollo-s-slok-sees-no-signs-of-ai-payoff-outside-tech-fLQspDMbaK8IVg0iIFA0), contrasted rising revenue per worker and profit margins among the Magnificent Seven with broadly flat results across the remaining S&P 500 companies. He argued that the broader corporate payoff remained difficult to see despite the investment in AI.

The more revealing organizational picture comes from [Hastings, Syverson and Zimmerman’s *The Deployment Failure Holding Back Your AI Investments*](https://hbr.org/2026/10/the-deployment-failure-holding-back-your-ai-investments), published in *Harvard Business Review* on October 21, 2026. Their survey of 300 individual contributors and frontline managers at large US companies found that respondents allocated 37% of their working time to coordination, compared with 44% to substantive work and 18% to administration. All respondents already used AI. The deployment problem was therefore visible among people who had adopted the technology, not merely among those waiting to try it.

The authors distinguish the labour spent coordinating from the economic losses created by fragmented decisions. Information is assembled and reconciled across functions, while each department acts on its own view of the business. Decisions that make sense locally can damage the enterprise as a whole. Their proposed response reaches into responsibilities, cross-functional deployment and incentives, rather than stopping at more productive individual tasks.

Consider a product that appears attractive in a sales report. Its manufacturing complexity, inventory requirements, returns and effect on other products may tell a different story. Sales, production and finance can each produce accurate reports while the enterprise makes a poor decision about whether to retain it. The missing capability is a reliable way to bring those consequences together and act on the complete picture. Faster report-writing leaves that problem substantially untouched.

This is why the coordination burden deserves more attention than the meeting calendar alone. Some of its cost appears as paid hours spent reconstructing context. Some appears as elapsed time while a decision waits. Some never appears as an identifiable expense because it takes the form of an improvement nobody could authorize, a customer problem nobody owned or a product decision made without its full economic consequences. An organization can become much better at producing information without becoming correspondingly better at using it.

Slok’s observation and the Hastings analysis therefore belong in the same discussion. One asks where the financial return has gone; the other directs attention to the system through which that return must be realized. The conclusion I draw is that companies should examine the organization consuming their new capability before concluding that the capability itself has little value. An unchanged decision system is a poor instrument for testing the potential of a changed production system.

## The unit of work has changed

The distinctive claim of the Coordination Shift is that AI changes the scale of work a person or small group can own. The gain is larger than faster execution of a narrow task. When analysis, implementation, verification and revision can remain within a coherent human–AI unit, some of the handoffs previously needed to combine those activities disappear. That changes which organizational boundaries are worth paying for.

Experimental evidence now gives that proposition a concrete basis. In [Dell’Acqua and colleagues’ *The Cybernetic Teammate*, published in *Organization Science* in 2026](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.20702), 791 Procter & Gamble professionals worked on product-innovation challenges. Individuals using AI matched the performance of two-person teams without it. AI-assisted participants also produced more balanced technical and commercial proposals, crossing distinctions that otherwise followed professional specialization. Some benefits obtained through collaboration could be reproduced within an individual’s augmented work.

The organizational boundary appears just as clearly in [Dillon and colleagues’ *Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI* (2025)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/shifting-work-patterns-with-generative-ai/). Their six-month randomized experiment covered approximately 6,000 knowledge workers. Workers who used the tool spent around three fewer hours per week on email, while time in meetings did not change significantly. The researchers found greater changes in activities individuals could alter independently than in activities requiring coordination with others.

Those findings describe an expanding capacity to act inside an arrangement that is harder to change. An employee can finish the analysis sooner without gaining the authority to implement its conclusion. An engineer can build a solution without changing the process for obtaining data, agreeing ownership or releasing it. The organization receives more completed contributions, but the contributions still wait to become a completed outcome.

The industrial division of labour rests partly on a trade: specialization improves performance, while coordination pays for bringing the specialists’ contributions together. As AI expands the useful range of individual expertise, that trade must be recalculated. A boundary established because several people were once required for a piece of work does not remain economically justified merely because it has acquired a manager, a budget and a process description.

This is where an ordinary AI programme can miss the larger opportunity. It begins with existing jobs and asks how to accelerate their tasks. The more important question is which tasks, dependencies and divisions are still necessary when those capabilities are combined differently. Helping six departments communicate faster can be valuable. Enabling a coherent unit to complete the work without four of those exchanges changes the operating model itself.

## Why the company keeps paying

There is no automatic mechanism that turns this mismatch into immediate corporate failure. A company can cover its costs, retain its customers and satisfy its owners while operating far below its available productive capacity. Contracts, switching costs and established relationships can protect an incumbent whose competitors carry similar overhead. A more capable entrant must still make its offer accessible and credible to buyers. Technical superiority does not abolish the customer's procurement process.

Persistent inefficiency has an empirical history. In [Bloom and colleagues’ *Does Management Matter? Evidence from India* (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjs044), a randomized management intervention increased productivity in textile plants by 17% during the first year. The researchers found that informational barriers had impeded adoption of profitable practices, while limits on managerial time constrained expansion and reallocation sufficiently that competition had not forced badly managed firms to exit. Available improvement and market survival were compatible with continued underperformance.

Inside a large enterprise, the old arrangement has another advantage: it is already authorized. Maintaining established headcount, reporting routines and approval chains often requires less explicit justification than changing them. A new AI subscription has a visible price and an owner expected to demonstrate its return. The accumulated cost of people waiting on one another is dispersed through existing budgets. Management can scrutinize the new expense while treating the larger inherited expense as an unavoidable condition of doing business.

The incentives can reinforce the arrangement without anyone deliberately choosing waste. A department protects its budget, a manager protects a commitment, and a specialist avoids accepting responsibility beyond their mandate. Each decision can be defensible within its own boundary. The enterprise pays for the combined result. Asking those same people to become more collaborative does not change the distribution of authority or the measures against which they are judged.

Syverson’s earlier work supplies an important connection. In [*The Productivity J-Curve* (2021), with Erik Brynjolfsson and Daniel Rock](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20180386), he examined the complementary investments needed to realize value from general-purpose technologies, including intangible investments whose contribution is poorly reflected in conventional measures. That is an argument for doing the organizational work, not for assuming the passage of time will do it. Waiting for returns and building the conditions that produce returns are different activities.

The consequence is uncomfortable but practical: companies may continue paying the coordination cost for as long as their economics permit it. The choice is not necessarily between immediate transformation and imminent bankruptcy. It is between using available capability deliberately and allowing it to dissolve into the existing structure. For employees who can see the unused possibilities, an acceptable operating margin does little to make the experience less absurd.

## Give the whole outcome an owner

The solution should begin where Hastings and colleagues locate the problem: at the boundaries between information, decisions and incentives. The goal is to make complete business judgments possible and give the people responsible a dependable path from judgment to action. Putting AI into each department independently leaves that path fragmented. Reorganizing around a coherent outcome makes the fragmentation itself something management must remove.

There is an established foundation for this approach. [Michael Hammer’s *Reengineering Work: Don’t Automate, Obliterate* (1990)](https://store.hbr.org/product/reengineering-work-don-t-automate-obliterate/90406) challenged companies using information technology merely to accelerate outdated processes. The Coordination Shift brings a specific new reason to revisit that challenge: the human–AI production unit can internalize work that an earlier organization had to distribute. The opportunity concerns both the process and the division of responsibility behind it.

My original analysis examined organizational models for their compatibility with this change. Team Topologies, platform organizations, networked or cellular structures and autonomous micro-enterprises offer useful foundations because they support local ownership, explicit boundaries and dependable shared capabilities. Their common contribution is to reduce the amount of recurring human negotiation needed to get work done. The appropriate arrangement follows the outcome and required competence, rather than an inherited minimum team size.

[Team Topologies’ interaction model](https://teamtopologies.com/key-concepts) distinguishes time-bounded collaboration, service provision and facilitation. That distinction is operationally useful. People may need to work intensively together to establish a capability; consuming the established capability should not require repeating that collaboration indefinitely. A shared data service, deployment platform or assurance capability earns its place by making other units more independent, not by turning every use into another ticket queue.

The practical precedents are tangible. In [REA Group’s account of its financial-services transformation](https://teamtopologies.com/industry-examples/evolving-an-empowered-high-performing-delivery-ecosystem-using-team-topologies-at-rea-group), teams became smaller, domain ownership clearer and decisions more decentralized. Its leaders reported doubled flow efficiency and improvements in first-time-right delivery. In manufacturing, [Haier’s account of its Rendanheyi development](https://www.haier.com/global/about-haier/culture/) describes a transition toward a network of micro-enterprises. Neither organizational autonomy nor alternatives to a conventional hierarchy need to be invented around a chatbot subscription.

Return to the product-range decision. A unit accountable for the economic health of one product family needs access to commercial, production, inventory and service information. It also needs a mandate to change defined aspects of the offering or operation within agreed limits. Domain expertise establishes what the information means; technical capability makes the relevant information and actions reliable; AI expands the analysis and execution they can perform together. The unit owns a result, not simply a report forwarded to somebody else.

That mandate must move real decisions. Which stock policies may the unit adjust? Which product variants may it propose retiring or retire within its authority? What customer obligations and financial limits govern the work? Who decides an exception, and how quickly? These questions turn autonomy from encouraging language into an operating arrangement. A team that must seek a fresh cross-departmental agreement for every meaningful action has responsibility without control.

Leadership remains responsible for enterprise direction, investment and trade-offs between outcomes. It must also prevent units from improving their own figures by exporting costs or risks elsewhere. Finance, security, architecture and other specialist functions help establish common constraints and provide expertise when it is needed. Their contribution becomes more valuable when it improves the conditions under which many decisions can be made, rather than repeatedly granting permission for substantially similar decisions.

Begin with one bounded outcome and a sponsor who can change the relevant mandates and budgets. Establish the current elapsed time, total effort, decision delays and business result. Give the unit the access, authority and shared services it needs, then remove the handoffs that the new arrangement replaces. Keeping every old approval while adding an empowered team simply creates a more expensive version of the original organization.

Evaluate the complete result: customer service, contribution, quality, recurrence of problems and full operating cost, including AI use and verification. Decide how released capacity will be used. It might support additional business, stronger reliability, lower expenditure, learning or better working conditions. Capacity has not become value merely because a dashboard reports hours saved. Management must connect the improvement to a deliberate use.

The transition also needs continuity. Shared records, documented decisions, executable checks, practiced recovery and apprenticeship make capability transferable. A smaller unit should not mean permanent dependence on one exceptional person. As successful arrangements spread, retire superseded reporting obligations and reward managers for removing recurring work. The test is whether the new way of operating becomes ordinary, not whether an executive can repeatedly rescue it from the old one.

## Safety must be able to act

The same coordination problem has consequences beyond productivity. Safety work requires observations to reach someone who understands them and can cause an effective response. If evidence, judgment and authority sit behind separate queues, the organization can devote considerable effort to safety while failing to control a developing hazard. A documented concern is not a resolved concern, however complete its approval history.

[Leveson and Thomas’s *STPA Handbook* (2018)](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf) treats the timing of control actions as part of safety. An action can be unsafe because it arrives too late, in the wrong sequence or for the wrong duration. The implication for organizational design is direct: the path between recognizing danger and exercising control belongs to the safety system. Its delay cannot be dismissed as merely administrative.

Software-delivery research offers a concrete example. [DORA’s findings on change approval](https://dora.dev/capabilities/streamlining-change-approval/), drawing on its 2019 research, associate heavyweight external approvals with poorer delivery performance and find no evidence that more formal external review produces lower change-failure rates. Its guidance places peer review close to delivery and supports it with automated testing and monitoring. Additional approval is not synonymous with additional protection.

A useful governance architecture establishes boundaries before routine action is needed, enforces them during operation and preserves evidence for independent scrutiny. Permissions, verification, monitoring, containment and recovery should be available to the people accountable for the outcome. Human deliberation determines consequential risk choices and changes to those boundaries. Operational responders need the authority to stop or correct an activity within them, including when waiting would increase harm.

Slowing or stopping execution can be the correct control action. The dangerous delay is the inability to decide and enforce that action while exposure continues. An organization whose safety response depends on a chain of unavailable decision-makers has built latency into its protection. As more consequential activity becomes automated, that weakness can affect employees, customers and the public, not just the efficiency of an internal programme.

The requirement is therefore stronger than faster delivery. The organization needs reliable authority, current evidence and a response appropriate to the dynamics of what it operates. A safety function should be able to show how its controls reduce exposure and improve intervention. Adding more procedure while leaving those capabilities unchanged can increase the appearance of care without improving the reality of control.

## Stop financing the obstruction

The two articles that prompted this essay belong together. Slok describes an economy in which much of the anticipated corporate payoff remains difficult to see. Hastings, Syverson and Zimmerman direct attention to the coordination burden and fragmented decision-making inside firms already using AI. The Coordination Shift connects those observations to a change in what individuals and small units can accomplish. New capacity is entering an organization still arranged around older constraints.

The response is available in practical organizational design: coherent outcome ownership, authority where the relevant knowledge resides, shared services that remove dependencies, and governance capable of acting. These changes require leadership because they alter budgets, responsibilities and the justification for existing processes. They will not emerge automatically from better prompts or another model release. Companies have to choose whether increased capability changes the institution or is absorbed by it.

My frustration is not that every business has failed to become a different company in a year. It is that so much visible obstruction still receives less scrutiny than the people and tools trying to work through it. We are discussing an age of abundance while tolerating organizations that make useful action scarce. Better services, affordable solutions and less exhausting work require more than productive machines; they require institutions prepared to let that productivity reach the world. We cannot keep paying people to wait and expect abundance to emerge from the queue.
