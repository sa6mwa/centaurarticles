# The Coordination Trap

*Why capable people are waiting, and what companies must change.*

I am tired of watching capable people wait inside organizations that keep telling them to move faster. Over the past year, I have accumulated more examples of this than I care to recount. The most frustrating cases are those where the capability exists, the problem is understood, and progress still depends on a chain of permissions, meetings and disputes over ownership. For the people closest to the work, the distance between what could be accomplished and what the organization allows to happen becomes increasingly difficult to tolerate.

When I developed [*The Coordination Shift*](https://c89.systems/10x.pdf) in September 2025, I expected organizational adaptation to take time. More than a year later, what wears thin is the acceptance of preventable delay as an ordinary cost of doing business. We have increasingly capable people and machines, urgent problems worth solving, and organizations willing to pay for both the capability and the obstruction. Calling that situation an AI adoption challenge understates the problem. We need to change how work is organized, who can decide, and why so much useful activity requires permission from people further from the problem.

The frustration is sharpened by how concrete the alternatives have become. When you can investigate, build and verify something in less time than it takes to arrange the meeting about whether to begin, waiting stops feeling like an unavoidable feature of difficult work. It starts revealing the cost of an organizational choice. The missing ingredient is no longer always another specialist, a larger budget or a better model. Sometimes it is permission to finish a coherent piece of work without handing it through an institution designed to split it apart.

## Faster work, unchanged bottlenecks

The gap now appears plainly in enterprise research. In [McKinsey’s *The State of AI in 2026*](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai), 80% of respondents reported improvements in their own productivity, but only 37% reported a positive AI contribution to their company’s earnings before interest and tax. That second figure was essentially unchanged from the previous year. More people were gaining capability without a corresponding increase in the proportion reporting a company-wide financial return.

The financial contrast is also visible in [Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok’s October assessment, reported by Barron’s](https://www.barrons.com/livecoverage/sunday1025/card/apollo-s-slok-sees-no-signs-of-ai-payoff-outside-tech-fLQspDMbaK8IVg0iIFA0): revenue per worker and margins were improving among the Magnificent Seven while remaining broadly flat across the other S&P 500 companies. The question this raises is what happens between a person becoming more capable and the organization receiving the benefit.

The answer was emerging just three days earlier. In [*The Deployment Failure Holding Back Your AI Investments* (2026)](https://hbr.org/2026/10/the-deployment-failure-holding-back-your-ai-investments), economists Justine Hastings, Chad Syverson and Seth Zimmerman surveyed 300 individual contributors and frontline managers working at large US companies. Respondents reported spending 37% of their working time on coordination—collecting information from different systems, reconciling conflicting figures, preparing recurring reports and aligning decisions across departments—compared with 44% on substantive work and 18% on administration. All of them were already using AI. Yet 76% wanted to spend less time coordinating, while 85% wanted more time for substantive work. The technology had reached the employees; the burden of organizational coordination remained.

The economists identify an even larger problem beneath those hours: decisions that are individually rational for a department can be economically damaging to the company as a whole. A cheaper freight choice may force another department to carry more inventory; a product may look profitable to finance while its production, stockholding and substitution effects make it costly to retain. Each function sees its own numbers, while assembling the complete picture is too slow or difficult to become routine. The company loses not only time spent coordinating but also the value of decisions it fails to make well. Hastings and colleagues recommend connecting work across functions and changing decision responsibilities and incentives accordingly. That takes the problem beyond AI adoption and directly into organizational design.

The distinction appears experimentally in [Dillon and colleagues’ *Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI* (2025)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/shifting-work-patterns-with-generative-ai/). In a six-month randomized experiment involving approximately 6,000 knowledge workers, people who used the AI tool spent about three fewer hours per week on email. Time spent in meetings did not change significantly. The researchers identified the distinction directly: activities individuals could change independently responded more readily than activities requiring coordination with others.

The production unit itself is changing. In [Dell’Acqua and colleagues’ *The Cybernetic Teammate*, published in *Organization Science* in 2026](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.20702), a randomized experiment with 791 Procter & Gamble professionals found that individuals using AI matched two-person teams without AI on product-innovation challenges. AI-assisted participants also produced proposals combining commercial and technical perspectives that otherwise tended to follow professional specializations. Some benefits previously obtained through human collaboration could be reproduced within one human–AI unit.

These findings describe the two sides of the Coordination Shift: an expanding unit of effective action and an organization whose dependencies have not necessarily changed around it. A capable person or small group can take responsibility for more complete work, while approvals, information access and competing priorities remain distributed across the old structure. Reports arrive earlier, prototypes accumulate sooner, and decisions still pass through the same queues.

If incoming work exceeds the capacity to review, decide and integrate it, faster production simply enlarges those queues. Counting generated documents, completed tasks or code commits can then make the organization look more productive while its unfinished work grows. The relevant output is something verified, integrated and useful, not another item awaiting permission to become useful.

[DORA’s 2025 research](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/) reaches a compatible conclusion: AI amplifies the strengths and weaknesses of the organizational system into which it is introduced. Improving the tools alone leaves that system intact. An assistant that helps six departments exchange information faster may save time; redesigning the work so that four of those exchanges are unnecessary removes recurring work and the waiting associated with it.

## A company can afford to waste its capability

The division of labour is a design decision, not a permanent law. Specialization earns its place when its benefits exceed the cost of integrating separate contributions. As AI expands what individuals can accomplish, that calculation changes. An organization should have to justify a boundary that repeatedly interrupts useful work, rather than require employees to justify every attempt to work across it.

Consider an order discrepancy passing through sales, finance, operations and IT. Sales knows the agreement, operations knows what was delivered, finance knows what was invoiced, and IT can investigate the mismatch between systems. Each department could handle its contribution efficiently while the customer waits for the case to travel between them. Giving everyone AI might improve every local metric without resolving the underlying problem: responsibility for the complete outcome is scattered across people who cannot act without one another.

The losses extend beyond waiting. A recurring fault may remain unfixed because the team that would pay for correcting it is not the team absorbing the operational cost. A valuable exception may be rejected because nobody has authority over all its consequences. The company pays to maintain the fragmentation, pays again to coordinate around it, and loses opportunities that never become anyone’s approved work.

Inside the company, standing still has the considerable advantage of already being budgeted. Existing roles, reporting routines and approval processes need no fresh business case each month. Removing them requires decisions that cross the very boundaries causing the delay. A manager may be able to approve an AI subscription while lacking authority to eliminate even one recurring dependency.

The incentives are visible in [Microsoft’s 2026 Work Trend Index](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization), which surveyed 20,000 AI-using workers across ten countries. Some 45% said it felt safer to focus on current goals than redesign how work gets done with AI. Only 13% said they were rewarded for reinventing work with AI even when results were not achieved. An organization can exhort its people to experiment while making the existing routine the safer career decision.

Competition need not correct this promptly. In [Bloom and colleagues’ randomized study of Indian textile plants, *Does Management Matter?* (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjs044), adopting better management practices increased productivity by 17% in the first year. Information barriers had helped keep those practices out, and competitive selection had not eliminated badly managed firms. Managerial constraints limited expansion by better-run competitors. Profitable improvements had been available without the market forcing their adoption.

For an incumbent protected by contracts, switching costs or established relationships, unused capacity can similarly coexist with acceptable profits. A faster entrant still needs distribution, trust and a route through customer procurement. Superior production economics become competitive pressure when customers can buy and adopt the alternative. Until then, the incumbent may continue earning sufficient returns while its employees wait.

[Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson’s *The Productivity J-Curve* (2021)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20180386) explains why general-purpose technologies need complementary investments in processes, skills and other intangible assets before their benefits fully appear in productivity measures. That is a reason to do the organizational work, not a reason to assume that time will do it. A company should be able to identify what it is changing beyond the tools it buys.

A person finishing earlier has released capacity, not automatically created a financial return. The organization can use that capacity for another outcome, better quality, learning, resilience or improved working conditions. Deliberate reserve capacity is useful; involuntary waiting behind an avoidable dependency is something else. Continued profitability can conceal the difference for years.

## Reorganize around outcomes

The intellectual starting point is older than generative AI. [Michael Hammer’s *Reengineering Work: Don’t Automate, Obliterate* (1990)](https://store.hbr.org/product/reengineering-work-don-t-automate-obliterate/90406) challenged using computers to accelerate obsolete business processes. AI makes that question newly urgent because the range of work one capable unit can complete has expanded. The design should begin with the outcome and the capabilities now available, rather than with a promise to preserve every existing role and handoff.

The organizational models evaluated in [*The Coordination Shift*](https://c89.systems/10x.pdf) provide foundations for that redesign: clear ownership, local authority, dependable interfaces and the ability to adapt without negotiating each change through a hierarchy. Team Topologies, platform organizations, cellular structures and autonomous micro-enterprises offer different ways of arranging these properties. Their value lies in the work they allow people to complete, not in adopting another organizational vocabulary.

The current enterprise evidence points in the same direction. [McKinsey’s 2026 survey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai) found fundamental workflow redesign reported by nearly three-quarters of respondents in its high-performing group, compared with one-quarter of others. The distinction was how work had changed, not simply whether AI was present.

[Team Topologies](https://teamtopologies.com/key-concepts) distinguishes focused collaboration for discovery, consuming a dependable service and receiving temporary expert assistance. These are different relationships with different costs. A routine dependency should not require the intensity of collaboration needed to discover something new. A reliable service allows the consuming unit to proceed; expert assistance helps it acquire a capability rather than remain permanently dependent on the expert.

In [REA Group’s account of its implementation](https://teamtopologies.com/industry-examples/evolving-an-empowered-high-performing-delivery-ecosystem-using-team-topologies-at-rea-group), its financial-services business moved from teams of up to 25 people toward units of roughly four to eight, with clearer domains and delegated decisions. Its leaders reported that flow efficiency—the share of elapsed delivery time spent actively working—doubled. They changed the conditions under which work could proceed, rather than demanding that people work harder inside the same conditions.

The precedent extends beyond software. [Haier’s account of its organizational evolution](https://www.haier.com/global/about-haier/culture/) describes a progression from hierarchical structures toward self-managing units and a network of micro-enterprises under Rendanheyi. That transformation began before the current AI era. Small units with substantial responsibility are an established organizational possibility, including within large industrial enterprises.

The same [Hastings, Syverson and Zimmerman analysis](https://hbr.org/2026/10/the-deployment-failure-holding-back-your-ai-investments) offers practical illustrations. At Amazon, a unified analytical system reduced some cross-functional workforce studies from months to days; the authors also describe an Uber financial report reduced from two days to ten minutes through an agent-supported workflow. What happens next is an organizational decision: when assembling the answer becomes inexpensive, someone must have the mandate to act on it. Otherwise, the report arrives in minutes and waits in the same queue.

The practical move is to place responsibility, information and authority together around a coherent outcome. For the order-discrepancy example, a unit could combine commercial and financial knowledge with technical delivery capability, using AI to extend its capacity to investigate and implement improvements. It would own resolution from discovery to confirmed correction, including preventing recurrence. Its composition should follow the expertise, continuity and operational coverage the outcome requires.

Ownership must come with a usable mandate. Specify which records the unit may access, which decisions it may make, what limits apply and which exceptions require independent judgment. Give it direct contact with the people experiencing the problem. A responsibility statement without the power to obtain information or act is merely a convenient location for blame.

Leadership owns priorities, investment choices and conflicts between legitimate objectives. Finance, security, legal and architecture specialists help establish shared constraints and resolve consequential exceptions. Their expertise should improve the boundaries within which work happens, rather than be repeatedly consumed by permission requests that could already have been settled.

Shared platforms make the arrangement practical at scale. Trusted data, identity, infrastructure, deployment and operational evidence should be dependable capabilities available through clear interfaces. A platform that requires a succession of tickets and negotiations for ordinary work has recreated the coordination problem behind a technical façade. Judge it by how reliably it enables others to proceed, including how quickly it resolves failures.

Relationships between units need the same clarity: what each provides, what the other may rely on, who owns failures and how changes are introduced. Enterprise-level leadership must see costs and risks that individual units would otherwise export to one another. Autonomy becomes productive when the surrounding system makes independent action coherent. The objective is to reduce recurring negotiation while preserving the information and authority needed to resolve real conflicts.

## Safety cannot wait in the same queue

Safety work is vulnerable to the coordination trap because its effectiveness depends on more than the quality of a review. Someone must observe the relevant state, understand what has changed, decide whether intervention is necessary and have authority to act. If those responsibilities are separated by slow handoffs, an organization can produce extensive safety documentation while leaving a known hazard unresolved.

This is an established safety-engineering problem. [Leveson and Thomas’s *STPA Handbook* (2018)](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf) explicitly treats the timing of control as part of safety: an otherwise appropriate action can become unsafe when it arrives too late or in the wrong sequence. Missing or delayed feedback can leave decision-makers acting on an incorrect understanding of the system. The organizational path between observation and intervention is therefore part of the control system, not administrative scenery around it.

Software-delivery research provides a concrete example. [DORA’s work on change approval, drawing on its 2019 research](https://dora.dev/capabilities/streamlining-change-approval/), found poorer delivery performance with heavyweight external approvals and no evidence that more formal external reviews were associated with lower change-failure rates. Its guidance favours peer review within delivery, automated testing and continuous monitoring. The presumed protection offered by administrative distance was not reflected in the results.

Effective control should be available where and when it is needed. Permissions constrain what can happen. Verification and independent checks establish evidence about changes. Monitoring reveals failures, containment limits their consequences, and rehearsed recovery makes restoration possible. The people accountable for the service need access to that evidence and authority to stop or correct an operation within predefined limits.

The STPA handbook also places importance on whole-system oversight, independent safety authority and direct access to safety information. The organizational objective is to keep that oversight informed and effective while giving operational responders the authority to act. Establishing risk boundaries may require careful deliberation; enforcing an established boundary must not depend on repeating that deliberation during an incident.

When the time available to prevent harm is shorter than the institution’s response cycle, coordination latency becomes part of the hazard. Recurring findings should therefore produce better controls, clearer authority and faster access to evidence. Adding another approval stage without fixing the underlying failure can lengthen the period of exposure. For businesses and essential services alike, documenting a danger and controlling it are different achievements.

## Change one outcome, then change the organization

Start with an important outcome that repeatedly crosses departmental boundaries. Order discrepancies, supplier onboarding or a recurring service failure can expose the problem without requiring an immediate corporate reorganization. Appoint a sponsor able to change the responsibilities involved. The experiment must test a different way of operating, not merely demonstrate that an AI tool can perform a task.

[Steve Blank’s essay on *Innovation Theater* (2019)](https://medium.com/@sgblank/why-companies-and-government-do-innovation-theater-instead-of-actual-innovation-711427bf3b2a) describes the familiar failure: workshops and initiatives multiply while procurement, personnel, security and other established processes continue to block delivery. An AI programme can reproduce that pattern perfectly. A showcase that survives on executive exceptions demonstrates what is possible while leaving the ordinary organization unable to do it.

Trace a representative set of cases from the initial need to the confirmed result. Separate useful work from waiting, repeated information gathering and rework. Record where decisions stall, which systems disagree and who can authorize the next action. This establishes a baseline for elapsed time, total effort, quality and customer impact, while revealing whether the obstacle is missing expertise, inaccessible information or fragmented authority.

Then transfer ownership of the outcome and the corresponding decision rights. Supply the information, shared capabilities and safeguards the unit needs. Remove the old handoffs that this arrangement replaces. Running a new unit underneath the old approval chain creates an additional layer, not a different operating model. The organizational intervention should be as concrete as the technical one: named decisions move, access changes and redundant gates disappear.

Measure results from the customer’s or operation’s perspective. A case completed faster is useful when it is correctly resolved and stays resolved. Track recurrence, rework, operational failures and full cost, including machine use and verification. Count work displaced into supporting functions, so that a unit cannot improve its figures by exporting effort elsewhere. Compare complete outcomes, not the volume of generated material or the enthusiasm of early adopters.

Funding must follow the same boundary. If the improvement budget sits in IT while the benefits appear in finance and operations, a sound enterprise investment can fail each departmental business case. Give the sponsor responsibility for the complete economic result. Reward leaders for removing recurring work and making shared capabilities dependable, rather than preserving the activity that supports their current budgets.

Make the destination of released capacity explicit. A programme that immediately treats every disclosed improvement as a staffing-cut opportunity gives employees a reason to conceal useful discoveries. Capacity can clear backlogs, improve reliability, support new business, avoid further expenditure or improve working conditions. Choosing its use is part of management’s job; leaving people blocked and then questioning the value of their tools is not a strategy.

Build continuity alongside capability. Shared records, executable checks, documented decisions and practiced recovery make work transferable. Apprenticeship and supervised ownership develop the next group of capable operators. A small unit should be able to maintain its responsibilities through leave, incidents and turnover. Removing unnecessary dependencies should make people more effective, not make one person permanently indispensable.

Expansion should change ordinary operations. Reuse the information access, controls, interfaces and ownership arrangements that worked. Retire superseded reporting and approval obligations instead of leaving them available to grow back. As more outcomes move into the new arrangement, management’s role shifts toward maintaining coherence, allocating resources and improving the shared system. Success is reached when useful work no longer requires an exceptional route around the organization.

## Stop paying to stand still

The Coordination Shift concerns the economics of useful work. Its central question is how much separation, handoff and supervision an outcome still requires when the capabilities available to each person have expanded. An organization that never revisits that question can consume extraordinary technical progress while preserving a thoroughly ordinary rate of improvement. It can even remain profitable while doing so.

That makes adaptation a leadership decision. Companies can continue financing their inherited division of labour, or they can give capable people ownership of outcomes, remove dependencies that no longer justify their cost and build controls that support timely action. Established organizational practice supplies usable foundations, and current research makes the mismatch increasingly visible. Another round of AI licences will not make those decisions on management’s behalf.

My impatience comes from the useful work being left undone while we discuss how transformative the technology might become. Better services, more affordable systems and less exhausting administrative work are concrete possibilities, not rewards that arrive automatically with the next model release. An age of abundance requires institutions that can convert capability into improvements people actually experience. We cannot keep paying capable people to wait and expect abundance to emerge from the queue.
