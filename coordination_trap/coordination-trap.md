# The Coordination Trap

*When execution outruns the enterprise.*

## Abstract

Companies are buying AI while paying for the coordination that keeps capable people waiting. Slok's missing payoff and Hastings's survey expose the contradiction: the tools have changed, but the authority to act has not. How much longer will leadership finance the obstruction and blame the technology?

## Introduction

I am tired of watching capable people wait while the organizations employing them demand more productivity. The work is understood, the capability is available, and progress still disappears into permissions, meetings and negotiations over ownership. Companies pay for the people, the technology and the delay, then ask why AI has not improved the result.

I began developing *The Coordination Shift* in September 2025 because the economics of my own work had changed [@blomgren2025coordination]. A capable engineer working with AI could carry a substantially larger and more coherent body of work than the organizational models around us assumed. More than a year later, the obstruction is still treated as normal. When investigating, building and verifying a solution takes less time than arranging the meeting about whether to begin, the delay is a management decision.

Torsten Slok's account of the missing financial payoff and the survey by Justine Hastings, Chad Syverson and Seth Zimmerman bring that frustration into focus. Employees already using AI are still spending much of their working lives making the organization function [@slok2026payoff] [@hastings2026deployment]. Leadership cannot keep demanding the returns of a new production capability while preserving every dependency of the old one. The response must reach into who owns decisions, how information moves and what management is rewarded for maintaining.

## The missing return has an organizational address

In his October 24 assessment, Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok contrasts rising revenue per worker and profit margins among the Magnificent Seven with broadly flat results across the other 493 companies in the S&P 500. His question is whether cheaper intelligence will become higher growth and profitability across the wider corporate economy. He also points to judgment, context, accountability and trust as obstacles to translating technical capability into business results [@slok2026payoff]. Those are precisely the conditions an organization has to arrange around productive work.

Three days earlier, Hastings, Syverson and Zimmerman published *The Deployment Failure Holding Back Your AI Investments* in *Harvard Business Review*. They surveyed 300 individual contributors and frontline managers at US companies with annual revenue exceeding $500 million. Respondents reported spending 37% of their working time on coordination, compared with 44% on substantive work and 18% on administration. Every respondent used AI. Yet 76% wanted to spend less time coordinating, while 85% wanted more time for substantive work [@hastings2026deployment].

Their definition of coordination is concrete: assembling and cleaning information from different systems, reconciling conflicting numbers, refreshing recurring reports and aligning work across functions. The tools had reached the employees, but much of the effort required to make the enterprise intelligible to itself remained. An employee could summarize a document or draft an analysis while still reconstructing the underlying business situation by hand each time a decision was needed.

The authors distinguish that labour from a second economic cost: decisions that improve one department's results can damage the enterprise as a whole. Their product-portfolio example makes the mechanism clear. Finance sees a product variant that still earns a profit. Manufacturing sees costly production changeovers. Planning carries inventory against unpredictable demand, while marketing knows that many customers would accept a similar product if the variant disappeared. Each function holds a useful part of the answer. Assessing the combined effect requires work across all four, while keeping the product requires no such agreement. The organization repeatedly authorizes continuity by default [@hastings2026deployment].

That default is expensive even when every report is accurate. The company pays for repeated information assembly, for the time a decision waits, and for the consequences of decisions made with only part of the picture. Some losses never appear as a separate expense: an improvement nobody could authorize, a customer problem nobody owned, or a product whose full cost was never brought into the decision. Faster report-writing can leave all three untouched.

Hastings also describes the effect of making recurring analysis a shared system capability at Amazon. Workforce questions that had required months of data assembly and statistical work could be answered in days, leaving scientists and analysts more capacity for difficult cases. The article's recommendation follows through to responsibilities and incentives: leadership must fund work across functions, assign the decisions that become possible and judge the return across the firm [@hastings2026deployment]. Reducing coordination changes what people can do; management has to change what they are authorized and expected to do with it.

Slok's observation and the Hastings analysis therefore belong in the same discussion. One asks where the financial return has gone; the other directs attention to the system through which that return must be realized. My conclusion is that companies should examine the organization consuming their new capability before concluding that the capability itself has little value. An unchanged decision system is a poor instrument for testing the potential of a changed production system.

## The unit of work has changed

The distinctive claim of the Coordination Shift is that AI changes the scale of work a person or small group can own. When analysis, implementation, verification and revision can remain within a coherent human–AI unit, some of the handoffs previously needed to combine those activities disappear. The gain extends beyond faster execution of an individual task to the possibility of completing work that previously depended on several separate contributions.

Experimental evidence gives that proposition a concrete basis. In *The Cybernetic Teammate*, Dell'Acqua and colleagues studied 791 Procter & Gamble professionals working on product-innovation challenges. Individuals using AI matched the performance of two-person teams without it. AI-assisted participants also produced more balanced technical and commercial proposals, crossing distinctions that otherwise followed professional specialization. Some benefits obtained through collaboration could be reproduced within an individual's augmented work [@dellacqua2026teammate].

The organizational boundary appears just as clearly in Dillon and colleagues' *Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI*. Their six-month randomized experiment covered approximately 6,000 knowledge workers. Workers who used the tool spent around three fewer hours per week on email, while time in meetings did not change significantly. Activities individuals could alter independently changed more readily than activities requiring coordination with others [@dillon2025workpatterns].

Those findings describe an expanding capacity to act inside an arrangement that is harder to change. An employee can finish the analysis sooner without gaining authority to implement its conclusion. An engineer can build a solution without changing the process for obtaining data, agreeing ownership or releasing it. The organization receives more completed contributions, but the contributions still wait to become a completed outcome. Where review and decision capacity remain fixed, increasing the arrival rate can enlarge the queue.

The industrial division of labour rests partly on a trade: specialization improves performance, while coordination pays for bringing the specialists' contributions together. As AI expands the useful range of individual expertise, that trade must be recalculated. A boundary established because several people were once required for a piece of work does not remain economically justified merely because it has acquired a manager, a budget and a process description.

An ordinary AI programme can miss this opportunity by beginning with existing jobs and asking how to accelerate their tasks. The more consequential question is which tasks, dependencies and divisions are still necessary when those capabilities are combined differently. Helping six departments communicate faster can be valuable. Enabling a coherent unit to complete the work without four of those exchanges changes the operating model itself.

## Why the company keeps paying

A company can cover its costs, retain its customers and satisfy its owners while operating far below its available productive capacity. Contracts, switching costs and established relationships can protect an incumbent whose competitors carry similar overhead. A more capable entrant must still make its offer accessible and credible to buyers. Technical superiority does not abolish the customer's procurement process, so the pressure to reorganize may arrive much later than the ability to do so.

Persistent inefficiency has an empirical history. In *Does Management Matter? Evidence from India*, Bloom and colleagues found that a randomized management intervention increased productivity in textile plants by 17% during the first year. Informational barriers had impeded adoption of profitable practices, while limits on managerial time constrained expansion and reallocation sufficiently that competition had not forced badly managed firms to exit [@bloom2013management]. Available improvement and market survival were compatible with continued underperformance.

Inside a large enterprise, the old arrangement has another advantage: it is already authorized. Established headcount, reporting routines and approval chains often require less explicit justification than changing them. A new AI subscription has a visible price and an owner expected to demonstrate its return. The accumulated cost of people waiting on one another is dispersed through existing budgets. Management can scrutinize the new expense while treating the larger inherited expense as an unavoidable condition of doing business.

The incentives can reinforce the arrangement without anyone deliberately choosing waste. A department protects its budget, a manager protects a commitment, and a specialist avoids accepting responsibility beyond their mandate. A recurring fault may remain unfixed because the team that would pay for correcting it is not the team absorbing its operating cost. Each decision can be defensible within its own boundary. The enterprise pays for the combined result, and exhortations to collaborate leave the distribution of authority untouched. The coordination trap closes when removing these dependencies requires agreement from the same divided functions, each judged against the local commitments that made the dependencies expensive in the first place.

Syverson's earlier work supplies a useful connection. In *The Productivity J-Curve*, with Erik Brynjolfsson and Daniel Rock, he examined the complementary investments needed to realize value from general-purpose technologies, including intangible investments poorly reflected in conventional measures [@brynjolfsson2021jcurve]. That is an argument for doing the organizational work. The passage of time does not assign a decision owner, connect inaccessible information or remove a redundant approval. A company expecting the benefits of reorganization should be able to identify what it is actually reorganizing.

Companies may continue paying the coordination cost for as long as their economics permit it. That makes adaptation a leadership decision well before it becomes a condition of survival. For employees who can see the unused possibilities, an acceptable operating margin does little to make the experience less absurd. The institution can afford the delay while the useful work remains undone.

## Give the whole outcome an owner

The response should begin where Hastings and colleagues locate the problem: at the boundaries between information, decisions and incentives. A complete business judgment needs both a dependable picture of its consequences and a path to action. Giving each department a faster way to produce its contribution leaves that path fragmented. Reorganizing around a coherent outcome makes the fragmentation itself something management must remove.

There is an established foundation for this approach. Michael Hammer's *Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate* challenged companies using information technology merely to accelerate outdated processes [@hammer1990reengineering]. The Coordination Shift brings a specific new reason to revisit that challenge: the human–AI production unit can internalize work that an earlier organization had to distribute. The opportunity concerns both the process and the division of responsibility behind it.

My original analysis examined organizational models for their compatibility with this change. Team Topologies, platform organizations, networked or cellular structures and autonomous micro-enterprises offer useful foundations because they support local ownership, explicit boundaries and dependable shared capabilities [@blomgren2025coordination]. Their practical contribution is to reduce recurring negotiation. The appropriate arrangement follows the outcome and required competence, including continuity and operational coverage.

Team Topologies distinguishes time-bounded collaboration, service provision and facilitation [@teamtopologiesconcepts]. People may need to work intensively together to establish a capability; consuming the established capability should not require repeating that collaboration indefinitely. A shared data service, deployment platform or assurance capability earns its place by making other units more independent. If ordinary use still requires a succession of tickets and negotiations, the dependency has merely acquired a technical interface.

The precedents are tangible. In REA Group's account of its financial-services transformation, teams became smaller, domain ownership clearer and decisions more decentralized. Its leaders reported doubled flow efficiency and improved first-time-right delivery [@devine2026rea]. They changed the conditions under which work could proceed. Management should scrutinize those conditions with the same seriousness it applies to the performance of the people working inside them.

Return to the product-range decision. A unit accountable for the economic health of one product family needs access to commercial, production, inventory and service information. It also needs a mandate to change defined aspects of the offering or operation within agreed limits. Domain expertise establishes what the information means; technical capability makes the relevant information and actions reliable; AI expands the analysis and execution they can perform together. Responsibility continues through the resulting change and its observed business effect.

The mandate must move real decisions. Which stock policies may the unit adjust? Which variants may it retire within its authority? What customer obligations and financial limits govern the work? Who decides an exception, and how quickly? These questions turn autonomy into an operating arrangement. A unit that must seek fresh agreement across departments for every meaningful action has responsibility without control.

Leadership remains responsible for enterprise direction, investment and trade-offs between outcomes. It must prevent units from improving their own figures by exporting costs or risks elsewhere. Finance, security, architecture and other specialist functions help establish common constraints and provide expertise when it is needed. Their contribution becomes more valuable when it improves the conditions under which many decisions can be made, instead of repeatedly granting permission for substantially similar decisions.

Begin with one bounded outcome and a sponsor who can change the relevant mandates and budgets. Establish the current elapsed time, total effort, decision delays and business result. Give the unit the access, authority and shared services it needs, then remove the handoffs that the new arrangement replaces. Keeping every old approval while adding an empowered team simply creates a more expensive version of the original organization.

Evaluate the complete result: customer service, contribution, quality, recurrence of problems and full operating cost, including AI use and verification. Decide how released capacity will be used. It might support additional business, stronger reliability, lower expenditure, learning or better working conditions. Capacity becomes value through a deliberate use; a dashboard reporting hours saved cannot make that decision for management.

The transition also needs continuity. Shared records, documented decisions, executable checks, practiced recovery and apprenticeship make capability transferable. As successful arrangements spread, retire superseded reporting obligations and reward managers for removing recurring work. The test is whether the new way of operating becomes ordinary, with the authority and support to continue after the first sponsor turns their attention elsewhere.

## Safety must be able to act

The same coordination problem has consequences beyond productivity. Safety work requires observations to reach someone who understands them and can cause an effective response. If evidence, judgment and authority sit behind separate queues, an organization can devote considerable effort to safety while failing to control a developing hazard. A documented concern remains unresolved until the necessary action has taken effect.

Leveson and Thomas's *STPA Handbook* treats the timing of control actions as part of safety. An action can be unsafe because it arrives too late, in the wrong sequence or for the wrong duration [@leveson2018stpa]. The implication for organizational design is direct: the path between recognizing danger and exercising control belongs to the safety system. Its delay cannot be dismissed as merely administrative.

Software-delivery research offers a concrete example. DORA's findings on change approval, drawing on its 2019 research, associate heavyweight external approvals with poorer delivery performance and find no evidence that more formal external review produces lower change-failure rates. Its guidance places peer review close to delivery and supports it with automated testing and monitoring [@dorachangeapproval]. The value of a control depends on whether it prevents, detects or contains a harmful change.

A useful governance architecture establishes boundaries before routine action is needed, enforces them during operation and preserves evidence for independent scrutiny. Permissions, verification, monitoring, containment and recovery should be available to the people accountable for the outcome. Human deliberation determines consequential risk choices and changes to those boundaries. Operational responders need the authority to stop or correct an activity within them, including when waiting would increase harm.

Slowing or stopping execution can be the correct control action. The dangerous delay is the inability to decide and enforce that action while exposure continues. An organization whose safety response depends on a chain of unavailable decision-makers has built latency into its protection. As more consequential activity becomes automated, that weakness can affect employees, customers and the public. A safety function should be able to show how its controls reduce exposure and improve intervention, including how quickly a known hazard can be brought under control.

## Stop financing the obstruction

The two articles that prompted this essay belong together. Slok describes a corporate economy in which much of the anticipated payoff remains difficult to see. Hastings, Syverson and Zimmerman locate a substantial burden in coordination and fragmented decisions inside firms already using AI. The Coordination Shift connects those observations to a change in what individuals and small units can accomplish. New capacity is entering organizations still arranged around older constraints.

The response is available in practical organizational design: coherent outcome ownership, authority where the relevant knowledge resides, shared services that remove dependencies, and governance capable of acting. These changes require leadership because they alter budgets, responsibilities and the justification for existing processes. They will not emerge automatically from better prompts or another model release. Companies have to choose whether increased capability changes the institution or is absorbed by it.

My impatience comes from the useful work being left undone while we discuss how transformative the technology might become. Better services, affordable solutions and less exhausting work are concrete possibilities. We are discussing an age of abundance while tolerating organizations that make useful action scarce. Productive machines need institutions prepared to let that productivity reach the world. We cannot keep paying capable people to wait and expect abundance to emerge from the queue.
