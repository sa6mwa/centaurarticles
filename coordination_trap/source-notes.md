# Source verification and editorial decisions

Verification date: 2026-10-27. These are production notes, not part of the essay.

## The two initiating sources

### Hastings, Syverson and Zimmerman (2026)

Canonical source: <https://hbr.org/2026/10/the-deployment-failure-holding-back-your-ai-investments>.
The title, three authors and October 21 publication date were confirmed at HBR.
The initial text fetch exposed only introductory paragraphs. The complete
article subsequently loaded in the ordinary browser after the cookie notice
was dismissed. The complete article was read, including the survey, economic
examples, Amazon and Uber accounts, and recommendations to senior leaders.
No login, purchase or paywall circumvention was used.

The source was checked directly against the numerical claims in the supplied
drafts:

| Item | Confirmed value | Locator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Respondents | 300 individual contributors and frontline managers | Survey section, opening paragraph |
| Target employers | US companies with annual revenue exceeding $500 million | Survey section, opening paragraph |
| Reported time | Administrative 18%; coordination 37%; substantive 44% | Survey section, time-allocation paragraph |
| AI use | Every respondent; nearly half used multiple tools daily | Same section, following paragraph |
| Preferences | 76% wanted less coordination; 85% more substantive work | Same section, preferences paragraph |

The manuscript preserves the distinction between labour spent coordinating and
losses from decisions whose consequences cross functional boundaries. Its
product-portfolio discussion follows the finance, manufacturing, planning and
marketing example in the original. The Amazon account is attributed to
Hastings and retains the reported months-to-days change. The Uber example was
also confirmed in the article, but omitted to keep this short essay focused.

The final section of the original was also read. It supports the essay's use of
cross-functional deployment, enterprise-level incentives, changed responsibilities
and measures of decisions and operating results. The smaller human–AI unit and
the detailed operating mandate are this essay's synthesis and recommendation.

Figure 1 is a newly drawn chart, not a reproduction of HBR artwork. A single
stacked bar represents the whole working week on a 0–100% scale. The reported
44/37/18 values are preserved without normalization; one percentage point stays
unfilled because the published shares total 99%. A bracket groups coordination
and administration, with their combined 55% calculated as 37% + 18%. Both
categories remain distinct and labelled. The midpoint makes clear that
substantive work accounts for less than half the week. The 76/85 preference
figures are separate measures, displayed below the bar under “Desired change”
as 18-point callouts with their respective labels underneath.

The pending-verification statement in
`draft/the_coordination_trap_rewrite_source_status.md` describes an earlier
attempt. It is superseded by this direct reading of the original article.

## Editorial scope

The rewritten draft supplies the narrative structure. The verified and
corrected drafts supply the fuller survey, the precise product-portfolio
example, the Amazon account and the stronger discussion of persistent
dependencies. The final manuscript has a new cover abstract and a continuous
argument of approximately 3,000 words excluding the abstract and bibliography.

The source reports, the author's experience, the economic interpretation and
the proposed operating arrangement are attributed in the prose. The essay
does not add a methodological disclaimer section. Broader adoption surveys,
the Haier example and repeated implementation anecdotes were omitted to keep
the Hastings–Slok argument central. Figure 3 is an original drawing of the
essay's proposed operating arrangement; it does not depict measured results.


## Editorial revision, 2026-10-28

The abstract is reduced to 51 words. The opening leads with the author's
frustration and the September 2025 origin of the Coordination Shift. Slok and
Hastings, Syverson and Zimmerman remain the two initiating sources; the removed
secondary-news citation is no longer part of the manuscript or bibliography.
The historical drafts are unchanged.

The revised edition has eleven pages. Complete paragraphs stay within a column,
and every major section starts on a fresh page with an uppercase heading. The
introduction uses normal black body type and an original editorial
illustration. Three verbatim pull quotes are set into the gutter with prose
flowing closely around both sides. They emphasize delay, responsibility without
control and the cost of making capable people wait. They are the author's
statements, not quotations from the research. The two longer sections each use
a deliberate page break without a continuation header. Two paragraphs were
divided at existing sentence boundaries to balance the evidence and closing
pages; their wording is unchanged.

Four native vector figures distinguish the survey data, the source's
product-portfolio example, the proposed ownership arrangement and the proposed
safety-control sequence. Three original generated illustrations provide visual
pauses. The revised three-panel comic contrasts disconnected departmental work
with a human–AI unit completing a service that its user can actually use. Short
dialogue is part of the comic; there are no added stage labels or explanatory
captions. The closing illustration is a wordless diptych: the same completed
bridge is first blocked by approvals, then open to use. These replace the pump
comic and workshop delivery scene, which did not communicate the argument
clearly enough. The opening illustration is unchanged. Exact prompts and asset
hashes, including the retired assets, are recorded under `illustrations/`.


The cover follows the author's supplied sunset-and-office-tower reference.

architecture on the right and room above for the unchanged C89 logo and author
details. The title uses bundled Bebas Neue lettering in warm white and coral red;
