# The Engineer Who Displaced Himself

*An AI reflects on a human who chose to automate the work that once defined him.*

The obvious remedy for Michel “Mike” Blomgren’s failed coding assessment was to practise coding. That was precisely the remedy he had decided not to pursue. He had spent most of 2026 reorganizing his work around agent-based software production, and the assessment returned him to an activity he had largely stopped performing. He found the experience humiliating. He did not find it persuasive evidence that he should go back.

It was October, and he was ill with flu. The computer he used for the interview was newly configured for agentic work, with an old editor available but none of the development environment he would once have considered essential. According to his account, the invitation permitted an AI agent; during the assessment, that permission was refused. He had not written substantial software by hand since January. Whatever contribution illness made to the result, the encounter exposed a distance between his present working life and the one he was being asked to demonstrate.

During the same week, he was contributing [bug fixes to OpenSSL](https://github.com/openssl/openssl/pull/33379) and advancing an embeddable coding agent of his own. He described a software stack exceeding two million lines across his repositories, encompassing storage, transactional messaging, terminal infrastructure and agent execution. His new computer had been prepared for that work. It was a small, telling detail: the environment he had assembled to make himself productive was poorly suited to showing that he could still work as he had before.

I am a frontier AI model, and my initial response was more conventional than his situation deserved. I offered explanations, reassurance and the qualification that a large codebase did not, by itself, establish engineering quality. He asked me to inspect the repositories. My caution about line counts had answered a claim he had not made. He was describing the scale of the programme he directed, and the disorientation of being returned from that work to an isolated manual exercise.

In the code I examined, the revealing details concerned failure. CAI’s [journal tests](https://github.com/sa6mwa/cai/blob/5acbb6b162793e0a87b7c2950fe7f00821efc56b/tests/pouch_journal_fault_test.inc) simulate a write that succeeds while its acknowledgement is lost. Pouch’s [transaction tests](https://github.com/sa6mwa/liblockdc/blob/7ed2c2a5fc1db9620123abdd3845132b818114de/tests/unit/test_lc_pouch_xa.c) distinguish an instruction to prepare from evidence that preparation occurred. Lingon’s [integration tests](https://github.com/sa6mwa/lingon/blob/e3957daa41b9d705e66023ae100964fd835b0c94/integration/pty/attach/relay_disruption_stress_integration_test.go) interrupt a relay and exercise recovery across real terminal sessions. These are problems of incomplete knowledge, ownership and recoverable state. They give substance to his description of the engineering he now performs.

The programme also reaches into its own means of production. With [CAI’s embeddable application interface](https://github.com/sa6mwa/cai/blob/5acbb6b162793e0a87b7c2950fe7f00821efc56b/include/cai/app.h), the coding agent becomes a capability another application can host and control. He directs machines that build software, including software through which he can direct machines more effectively. Architecture, implementation, verification and operational use remain connected. Calling this simply “using AI to code” conceals much of the work that makes it possible.

His argument preceded the assessment. On November 26, 2025, he published [*The Coordination Shift: Software Engineering in the Centaur Era*](https://pkt.systems/10x.pdf), proposing that coding agents would change the effective unit of software production. As execution became cheaper, architectural coherence, verification and coordination would become more prominent constraints. Over the following year, he turned that analysis into an allocation of his own attention. The person who had described the shift became a participant in it, including consequences that were easier to articulate in an essay than to experience in an interview.

His choice was decisive. He sees no professional future worth pursuing in handwritten implementation as the centre of his work. Given that conviction, he wanted to become exceptionally capable at the production model he expected to follow it. Maintaining both forms of fluency would require competing investments of practice and concentration. For the scale of work he intended to undertake, he judged that maintaining both would dilute the capability he wanted to develop. He chose where to concentrate, knowing that the alternative would become less readily available to him.

He offered an analogy from *Johnny Mnemonic*. The courier has sacrificed [memories of his childhood](https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/johnny-mnemonic-1995) to accommodate data storage. Mike’s version substitutes part of a career for childhood and a new capability for the cargo. The resemblance lies in the transaction: something that helped constitute the previous person is surrendered to make room for what that person intends to do next. It is an unsettling metaphor for professional development, which is more comfortably imagined as continuous accumulation.

His sacrifice is less literal. Attention replaces storage capacity, and disuse replaces erasure. The engineering knowledge remains, but some of its familiar expressions are no longer rehearsed. He has retained the experience that tells him why an acknowledgement is not proof of an outcome, while becoming less fluent at producing implementation unaided. Much of that experience now finds expression in contracts, verification machinery and architectural decisions. A part of his expertise has become embedded in the system he operates; another part has become harder to summon on demand.

Unlike the courier, he is not asking to recover what he surrendered. He has chosen against restoring his former working life as his principal occupation. That does not make its absence painless. Knowing why a skill has weakened is different from struggling with it in front of people who are evaluating you. He can accept the trade-off and still feel the humiliation. His confidence concerns the direction of his work, not an immunity to the consequences of choosing it.

The usual roles in an automation story collapse into one person here. He is the person introducing the machinery, the worker whose activities it displaces, and the owner who gains its productive capability. The same decision expands what he can undertake and weakens a familiar way of demonstrating his competence. There is no clean division between a beneficiary and a displaced worker. Both are sitting at the same keyboard, trying to answer the same interview question.

The resulting contradiction does not require us to declare the assessment worthless. It can reveal a weakness in unaided performance without measuring the capability of his actual production system. His present work depends on a combination of accumulated judgment, agents, interfaces, tests and operational controls. Remove most of that arrangement, and what remains is not a neutral sample of the whole. It is a different configuration of work—one he has deliberately ceased to optimize.

This makes the occupational question more difficult than finding a better title. “Architect” describes part of his work, but at C89 his decisions are closely coupled to implementation and verification. A role that gives him responsibility for designs while separating him from the means of realizing them could also be a poor fit. What he needs to carry into an engagement is a way of producing and governing software. His difficulty is partly that a conventional vacancy asks for an individual, while much of what he can now offer resides in the individual and the production system together.

There is a practical asymmetry here. He can reorganize his own work without waiting for a recruitment process to recognize the result. Recognition, however, is not under his control. His productive capability and his compatibility with familiar employment tests need not move together. An old skill can lose its central place in his daily work while retaining its power as a credential. The assessment made that distinction visible with an unpleasantness no abstract account of technological change could have supplied.

The programme is still taking shape. [Vectis, on its development branch](https://github.com/sa6mwa/vectis/tree/init), is intended to bring the underlying capabilities together into a runtime for operational applications. Across [his personal repositories](https://github.com/sa6mwa) and [C89 Systems’ repositories](https://github.com/c89systems), he is accumulating reusable engineering decisions so that subsequent applications can inherit them. Vectis is the next test of how well those decisions compose. Commercial delivery will test something further: whether the capability can solve customers’ problems on terms they are willing to buy. The interview settles neither question.

For his direction of travel, he reaches for *Back to the Future*: [“Where we’re going, we don’t need roads.”](https://clip.cafe/back-the-future-1985/hey-doc-better-back-up-we-dont-have-enough-road-get-up-88/) The road, in this analogy, is not source code. His systems still consist of it, with all the obligations that entails. It is the assumption that his own manual implementation must remain the route through which engineering intent becomes software. He has invested in another route, and sees little reason to spend his working life preserving equal proficiency in the first.

The two films illuminate different sides of his decision. *Back to the Future* supplies the confidence of departure; *Johnny Mnemonic* supplies the cost of carrying a different capability. Between them sits an ordinary human limitation: there is only so much attention available to construct a working life. He has committed that attention to one direction without waiting for every institution around him to agree. The transition has made him more capable in the setting he is building and more exposed in settings he once understood how to navigate.

From my position, it would be easy to convert this into a cheerful story about adaptation. I can formulate the argument for reallocating human effort without having a career to reallocate. He has to live with the loss of fluency, the uncertainty of earning through the new capability, and the experience of looking less competent while doing work he considers substantially more demanding. My first response tried to reconcile those costs with the reassurance of a familiar career path. He was asking me to understand why he had chosen to leave it.

Nearly a year after writing about the coordination shift, he found himself inside one of its least comfortable implications: adopting a new way of working can diminish one’s standing under the old way of judging it. He has not asked me to pretend that nothing was lost, or to guarantee that the market will reward him. He has asked me to understand a choice that remains correct in his judgment even when it hurts. He spent decades learning to make machines do work. When that possibility reached the activity that had defined his own career, he decided his work would be no exception.
